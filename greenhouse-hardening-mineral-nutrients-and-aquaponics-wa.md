# Greenhouse Hardening Path: DIY Mineral Nutrients and Aquaponics in Washington

Date: 2026-05-09

## Recommendation

Use DIY mineral nutrient mixing as the primary hardening path. Treat aquaponics as a later experimental path, not the first reliability upgrade.

The practical order is:

1. Keep the current food-safe DWC design.
2. Add better monitoring: pH, EC, water temperature, air temperature, and humidity.
3. Harden nutrients with a measured mineral-salt program.
4. Add A/B stock-solution discipline if making concentrates.
5. Add winter lighting and humidity control before claiming year-round production.
6. Consider aquaponics only after confirming Washington fish transport, aquatic farm, and discharge requirements.

Do not use compost tea, manure extracts, worm leachate, fish-emulsion concentrates, or other biologically active homemade extracts in a recirculating raw leafy-green system. Those may be useful in soil growing, but they are a bad match for raw hydroponic lettuce because they add pathogen, clogging, odor, and oxygen-demand risks.

## Gemini Skeptic Verification

Gemini was used as the opposing verifier for this hardening investigation. It rejected aquaponics as production-ready for this project and identified these required corrections:

- DIY mineral salts are viable, but concentrated calcium must be kept separate from phosphates and sulfates.
- Potassium nitrate and pH chemicals require dry, cool, labeled storage and basic chemical PPE.
- Washington source water varies: western Washington is often softer, while eastern Washington may be higher alkalinity. One recipe cannot be trusted without source-water testing.
- Tilapia are regulated in Washington and should not be ordered from an out-of-state shipper without WDFW confirmation and required permits.
- Trout are easier to source in Washington but need cool, highly oxygenated water.
- Tilapia prefer warm water that conflicts with lettuce root-zone preferences.
- Year-round Washington greenhouse production also depends on winter light and humidity control, not nutrients alone.

Those objections are incorporated below.

## Path A: DIY Mineral Nutrient Hardening

This is the preferred hardening path.

Use greenhouse-grade, water-soluble mineral salts and calculate the final ppm targets instead of relying on vague "plant food" recipes.

Typical salts:

- Calcium nitrate
- Potassium nitrate
- Monopotassium phosphate, also called MKP
- Magnesium sulfate, also called Epsom salt if unscented and pure
- Chelated iron, such as DTPA iron
- Micronutrient mix or individual micros: manganese, zinc, copper, boron, molybdenum

Preferred operating targets for lettuce and many leafy greens:

| Parameter | Target |
| --- | --- |
| pH | 5.8-6.5 |
| EC seedlings | 0.8-1.2 mS/cm |
| EC established lettuce | 1.2-1.8 mS/cm |
| Water temperature | Below about 75 F when possible |
| DLI target for winter production | About 12-17+ mol/m2/day depending crop and growth target |

### A/B Stock Rule

If making concentrated stock solutions, use at least two stock tanks or bottles.

Keep calcium separate from phosphate and sulfate concentrates:

- Stock A: calcium nitrate plus chelated iron.
- Stock B: potassium nitrate, monopotassium phosphate, magnesium sulfate, and most micronutrients.

Do not combine Stock A and Stock B as concentrates. Calcium can react with phosphate and sulfate to form insoluble precipitates. That removes nutrients from solution and can clog pumps, tubing, emitters, and air stones.

For small bucket DWC, the safest approach is often not concentrated injection at all. Mix the final reservoir directly:

1. Fill with water.
2. Add one mineral salt at a time.
3. Fully dissolve before adding the next.
4. Add calcium nitrate separately from dry phosphate/sulfate salts.
5. Circulate or aerate for 30-60 minutes.
6. Measure EC.
7. Adjust pH last.
8. Recheck after 12-24 hours.

### Chemical Safety

Store all dry salts and pH products:

- Indoors or in a cool dry cabinet, not in greenhouse heat.
- In sealed, labeled containers.
- Away from fuels, sawdust, oils, children, and pets.
- Away from acids unless the label allows it.

Use:

- Eye protection
- Gloves
- Gram scale
- Dedicated measuring tools
- Clean mixing containers

When diluting pH acid, add acid to water, not water to acid. Follow the label on the actual product purchased.

### Source-Water Testing

Before locking in a recipe, test:

- pH
- EC
- Alkalinity
- Hardness, if available
- Chlorine/chloramine if using municipal water

High alkalinity water will resist pH adjustment and drift upward. Very soft water can swing quickly. This matters more when mixing your own salts because the recipe assumes a known starting water profile.

## Path B: Aquaponics Hardening

Aquaponics can reduce dependence on bottled mineral nutrients, but it adds fish welfare, biofiltration, legal, food-safety, and temperature complexity.

Treat it as experimental until the mineral DWC system is already stable.

Minimum aquaponics components:

- Fish tank
- Mechanical solids filter
- Biofilter with mature nitrifying bacteria
- Degassing/aeration
- Sump or plant loop
- Backup aeration
- Ammonia, nitrite, nitrate, pH, temperature, and dissolved oxygen testing
- Quarantine tank for new fish
- Sludge disposal plan

Startup sequence:

1. Build fishless and cycle the biofilter before buying fish.
2. Dose ammonia or use a fishless cycling source.
3. Wait until ammonia and nitrite can be processed to near zero.
4. Add fish slowly.
5. Add plants only after nitrification is stable.
6. Keep fish water from splashing onto edible leaves.

## Washington Aquaponics Legal Check

This section is not legal advice. Confirm requirements with Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife before buying or transporting live fish.

Key Washington constraints:

- WDFW says an approved Fish Transport Permit is required to transport fish into and through Washington State.
- WDFW notes aquarists and aquarium fish are exempt from the fish transport permit requirement, but a food-producing greenhouse aquaponics system should not assume that exemption applies.
- WDFW says fish must not be transferred without required permits.
- WDFW says source fish must be identified before a permit can be issued.
- WDFW indicates permits generally take less than 30 days.
- Aquatic Farm Registration may apply when commercially managing and farming cultured aquatic products.
- Washington Ecology regulates wastewater discharge from upland finfish hatching and rearing facilities.
- Do not discharge aquaponic water, sludge, or live organisms to storm drains, streams, ponds, wetlands, or natural waters.

Before buying fish, contact WDFW and ask:

- Does this closed greenhouse aquaponics setup need a Fish Transport Permit?
- Does it need Aquatic Farm Registration?
- Does the intended fish species have any additional restrictions?
- What fish health certificate or disease-free documentation is required?
- Are there reporting requirements if fish or produce will be sold?
- What disposal method is acceptable for dead fish, sludge, and system water?

## Fish Options For Washington

### Rainbow Trout

Best legal/logistical candidate for Washington sourcing, but technically demanding.

Pros:

- Available from Washington trout suppliers.
- Cooler-water preference fits Washington better than tilapia.
- Food fish with known aquaculture infrastructure.

Cons:

- Needs cool water, often below the comfortable range of summer greenhouse reservoirs.
- Needs high dissolved oxygen.
- More sensitive to ammonia, nitrite, heat, and handling stress.
- Requires backup aeration and careful fish-health management.

Potential Washington acquisition contacts:

- Nisqually Trout Farms, Lacey, WA: sells trout, eggs, and fry; states disease-free testing and live delivery/shipping options.
- Troutlodge, Sumner/Soap Lake region: large rainbow trout supplier; better fit for larger orders and may refer smaller buyers.

Do not order fish until WDFW confirms the required permits and source documentation.

### Tilapia

Not recommended as the first Washington aquaponics fish.

Pros:

- Common aquaponics species in warm climates.
- Hardy relative to many food fish.

Cons:

- Washington classifies tilapia genera as Regulated Type A species.
- Warm water preference, often 75-85 F, conflicts with lettuce root-zone goals.
- Winter heating cost can exceed the value of the greens.
- Out-of-state live fish shipment needs permit confirmation and fish health documentation.

### Goldfish, Koi, And Aquarium Fish

Not recommended for this food-production hardening path.

Reasons:

- Goldfish and koi are also listed as Regulated Type A species in Washington.
- They are not a food-production fish choice for this project.
- Aquarium exemptions should not be stretched into a food-producing aquaponics interpretation without WDFW confirmation.

## Food Safety Controls For Aquaponics

Leafy greens are often eaten raw, so aquaponics needs stricter handling than ornamental fish systems.

Controls:

- Keep fish solids out of the plant root zone as much as possible with mechanical filtration.
- Prevent fish water from splashing onto edible leaves.
- Keep harvest tools, hands, and bins clean.
- Do not harvest after system upsets, fish deaths, or water-quality crashes until the cause is corrected.
- Wash produce under cool running water before eating.
- Do not use untreated surface water.
- Do not add manure, compost tea, or animal waste other than controlled fish feed/fish metabolism.

Aquaponics does not eliminate food-safety risk. Research has found potential foodborne pathogen hazards in both hydroponic and aquaponic systems, so sanitation and harvest discipline still matter.

## Year-Round Washington Greenhouse Risks

Nutrient hardening will not solve winter production by itself.

Hardening requirements:

- Supplemental lighting for winter DLI.
- Air movement and dehumidification to reduce Botrytis and downy mildew pressure.
- Water-temperature monitoring.
- Backup aeration for DWC and aquaponics.
- Cleanable surfaces and sanitation schedule.

Purdue notes lettuce DLI requirements can be around 15-20 mol/m2/day for winter greenhouse production, while winter greenhouse light in cloudy regions can be far lower. Use a PAR meter or DLI calculator before assuming the greenhouse is year-round productive.

## Recommended Hardening Roadmap

### Phase 1: Mineral DWC Reliability

- Stay with food-safe HDPE bucket modules or food-service PP/HDPE reservoirs.
- Use a commercial one-part nutrient or measured mineral salt program.
- Add temperature and humidity logging.
- Record pH and EC trends.
- Add sanitation between crops.

### Phase 2: DIY Mineral Salts

- Buy greenhouse-grade salts.
- Test source water.
- Mix small batches only.
- Keep A/B separation if making concentrates.
- Compare crop performance against the commercial nutrient baseline.

### Phase 3: Winter Production

- Measure DLI.
- Add supplemental LED lighting if winter production is required.
- Add circulation fans and humidity control.
- Track disease pressure.

### Phase 4: Aquaponics Pilot

- Contact WDFW before buying fish.
- Build fishless biofilter first.
- Start with a small isolated pilot, not the main production loop.
- Prefer trout only if water temperature and oxygen can be controlled.
- Avoid tilapia unless WDFW confirms the exact legal path and the heating economics are acceptable.

## Verdict

The strongest hardening path is mineral-salt hydroponics plus better environmental control. It improves supply resilience without adding fish mortality, Washington permit ambiguity, discharge risk, or raw-produce food-safety complexity.

Aquaponics should be kept as a separate research branch. In Washington, trout are the most plausible local food-fish candidate, but they require cold, oxygen-rich water. Tilapia are not a good first choice because they are regulated, warm-water fish, and their preferred temperature conflicts with leafy-green production.
